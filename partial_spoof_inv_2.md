# From cepstral joins to cloned voices: a technical history of synthetic speech and its forensic rebuttal

**The audio authenticity problem has inverted in thirty years.** In 1996 the hard problem was making a machine sound human; by 2026 it is proving a human actually spoke. Concatenative unit-selection synthesis — the Hunt–Black ICASSP paper that defined the field — treated naturalness as a graph-search problem over recorded speech fragments, with audible "seams" at every join. Neural vocoders eliminated those seams, and by doing so eliminated the forensic handles that editors and courts had relied on for a generation. The consequence, visible in the Fraunhofer AISEC "In-the-Wild" benchmark, is that detection systems trained in the lab degrade by **200% to 1,000% equal-error-rate** when confronted with real podcast and social-media deepfakes. This report traces how that inversion happened, what traces remain detectable, and why generalization — not raw accuracy — is now the field's defining problem.

## The Hunt–Black framework that defined concatenative synthesis

Andrew Hunt and Alan Black's 1996 ICASSP paper, *Unit Selection in a Concatenative Speech Synthesis System Using a Large Speech Database*, recast waveform synthesis as a state-transition decoding problem. The synthesis database is treated as a network in which **state-occupancy cost is the distance between a candidate unit and a target specification**, and the **transition cost is an estimate of concatenation quality between consecutive units**. A pruned Viterbi search then selects the optimal path — a framework structurally identical to HMM-based speech recognition, but run in reverse.

The two cost functions carry the entire quality burden. The target cost C^t(t_i, u_i) = Σ w_j^t C_j^t(t_i, u_i) measures how well a database unit matches the linguistic specification — phonetic identity, stress, position in phrase, and prosodic context such as mean log-power and mean F0. The concatenation (join) cost operates on the signal itself: Hunt and Black used three sub-costs, centered on cepstral distance at the join point, F0 difference, and power difference. Weights were originally trained from natural speech, though in practice the community tuned them by trial and error because automatic weight training proved brittle.

The framework's lasting contribution was not any single cost function but the **formal separation of linguistic appropriateness from acoustic continuity**. Subsequent work spent two decades refining the join cost. Kirkpatrick, O'Brien and Scaife (2006), Wouters and Macon (1998), and Pantazis and Stylianou (2005) compared spectral continuity measures; Kelly and Gobl (2011) showed that MFCC window length interacts with phoneme class — **25 ms is optimal for vowels, 45 ms for voiceless fricatives** — meaning the "best" join cost is phone-dependent. The ideal join cost, as the literature settled it, is one that correlates highly with human perception of discontinuity while using only measurable spectral, amplitude, and F0 properties of the candidate units.

## Why concatenative seams were forensically legible

The audible joins that haunted unit-selection synthesis were the same artifacts that made audio editing detectable. Both involve stitching waveforms recorded at different times, and both produce detectable phase, spectral, and noise-floor discontinuities. The forensic literature of the 2000s and 2010s essentially consists of formalized versions of the join-cost problem, repurposed to catch humans rather than guide machines.

The dominant approach became the **Electric Network Frequency (ENF) criterion**. Mains-powered recorders and microphones in electromagnetic fields unintentionally capture the 50 Hz or 60 Hz grid signal, whose frequency fluctuates uniquely over time and location — a natural timestamp. Nicolalde Rodríguez, Apolinário and Biscainho's 2010 *IEEE TIFS* paper, *Audio Authenticity: Detecting ENF Discontinuity With High Precision Phase Analysis*, reduced this to a single-tone phase-estimation problem: down-sample, band-filter around nominal ENF, then use high-precision DFT-based phase analysis. **Abrupt phase jumps mark insertions or deletions**, with the method achieving roughly **6% EER on clean signals**. Later extensions used ENF higher harmonics when the fundamental had been filtered out, ESPRIT-Hilbert estimators with SVM classifiers (the SPHINS scheme), and multi-harmonic wavelet decomposition over silent segments to catch edits that the fundamental alone missed.

Parallel approaches targeted other residuals. Cooper's AES paper on **butt-spliced edits** searches high-frequency waveform discontinuities where the ratio of edit energy to acoustic signal energy is most favorable. Zhao et al. and Capoferri et al. model **acoustic channel impulse response and reverberation time** as environmental fingerprints, catching splices between different rooms. Cuccovillo et al. (2013) used **microphone-classification inconsistencies** to flag mixed-source forgeries. Yang et al. localized splicing in MP3 audio through inconsistent frame offsets; Pan, Meng, and Yan exploited local noise-level variances and MFCC-variance jumps across syllables.

Every one of these methods shares a structural assumption: **the forgery is a concatenation of real recordings**, and therefore the join itself — whether in ENF phase, noise floor, reverberation, or microphone response — is a detectable seam. This assumption held for the entire era in which synthesis meant unit selection.

## How neural synthesis broke the seam-detection paradigm

Modern text-to-speech does not splice. WaveNet, Tacotron, and their descendants generate waveforms sample-by-sample from learned distributions; there is no recorded source material, no room, no microphone, no mains hum, no join. The entire forensic toolkit built around editing artifacts becomes inapplicable, because **there are no edits** — only a continuous synthetic signal that was never a physical acoustic event.

This is not a subtle shift. ENF detection requires that a grid signal be present to exhibit discontinuities; a neural vocoder produces no grid signal at all. Reverberation-based detection requires two rooms to disagree; a neural vocoder has no room. Microphone-classification forensics requires two microphones to differ; there is no microphone. The very absence of physical artifacts becomes a signature, but only if detectors are explicitly trained to notice their absence — which means the problem shifts from signal processing to statistical pattern recognition on synthesized waveforms themselves.

## The generalization cliff revealed by In-the-Wild

The definitive empirical study of this shift is Müller, Czempin, Dieckmann, Froghyar, and Böttinger's *Does Audio Deepfake Detection Generalize?* (Interspeech 2022). The authors re-implemented **twelve popular detection architectures** — including Meso-4, MesoInception, ResNet18, RawNet2, RawGAT-ST, LCNN, and Transformer variants — under uniform training and evaluation, then tested them on ASVspoof 2019 LA and on a new **37.9-hour "In-the-Wild" dataset** of found audio (17.2 hours deepfake, 20.7 hours bona fide) scraped from YouTube clips of celebrities and politicians.

The feature-extraction findings were sharp. **Replacing mel-spectrograms with constant-Q or log-spectrograms improved EER by 37% on average**, all other factors held constant. Raw-waveform end-to-end models outperformed spectrogram-based models, attributed to finer feature-extraction resolution. These results have since pushed the field toward raw, end-to-end architectures.

The generalization findings were damning. Models reached strong EERs on ASVspoof 2019 evaluation data but **degraded by 200% to 1,000% EER** on In-the-Wild audio, with some architectures collapsing toward random guessing. Critically, adding the ASVspoof 2019 eval split to training did not improve out-of-domain performance — **33.1 ± 0.2% EER** — because all ASVspoof splits derive from VCTK, a studio-recorded semi-anechoic corpus of professional speakers. The community had been tuning to a single laboratory benchmark.

Müller's 2024 follow-up, *Harder or Different? Understanding Generalization of Audio Deepfake Detection*, decomposed the performance gap into "hardness" (the deepfakes themselves being harder) versus "difference" (distributional mismatch). For ASVspoof 2021 and In-the-Wild, **"difference" dominated nearly exclusively**, compared to vision benchmarks like CIFAR-10 → CIFAR-10.2 where hardness and difference split roughly evenly. The paper identifies the "silence shortcut" as canonical: ASVspoof 2019 models achieve near-perfect performance using the length of leading silence, which correlates with class label but is semantically meaningless. Models learn artifacts of dataset construction rather than properties of synthesis.

The 2026 ML-ITW benchmark extends this critique cross-lingually: **14 languages, 7 social-media platforms, 180 public figures, 28.4 hours**, with English and Chinese dominating and substantial coverage of Japanese, German, Portuguese, and Korean. Results again show significant degradation versus both ASVspoof 2019 and the original English-only, YouTube-only In-the-Wild set — end-to-end neural models, self-supervised hybrids, and audio LLMs all exhibit language-dependent variability.

## What the three eras share and do not share

A comparison across the three detection regimes clarifies why the problem has become harder rather than simply different:

| Era | Synthesis/edit model | Core detectable trace | Dominant method | Typical reported EER |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-2010 analog/edit forensics | Splice of real recordings | ENF phase, butt-splice discontinuity, room impulse response | Phase analysis, high-pass filtering | ~6% (clean) |
| Concatenative TTS era | Unit selection from database | Cepstral, F0, power discontinuity at joins | Join-cost analysis, MFCC variance | Perceptually audible seams |
| Neural TTS / voice cloning | End-to-end waveform generation | Statistical artifacts of generative models | Raw-waveform CNN/Transformer, SSL features | 0.1–5% lab, 30%+ in-the-wild |

The through-line is that **every detection regime exploits a signature that the generator in the next regime eliminates**. ENF disappears when recording moves off-mains. Concatenative joins disappear when synthesis goes neural. Spectrogram artifacts specific to early vocoders are being smoothed away by diffusion-based and flow-matching vocoders. The pattern predicts that current SSL-based detectors, which rely on wav2vec 2.0 and Whisper embeddings to spot subtle distributional artifacts, will themselves degrade as synthesis models ingest more natural data and adversarial training.

## Why the forensic problem is now a distribution problem

The deepest finding across this literature is not about any specific detector but about the nature of the task. Classical audio forensics asked: **does this signal contain a physical discontinuity?** — a question answerable from first principles of signal processing. Modern deepfake detection asks: **was this signal drawn from the distribution of real recordings or from the distribution of generated recordings?** — a question that depends entirely on which generators and which real recordings define the distributions.

This is why the "difference" term dominates the generalization gap. A join-cost analysis based on MFCC distance works identically on every concatenated waveform because the underlying physics is the same. A RawNet2 classifier works only on the generators and acoustic conditions it has seen, because it has no physics to fall back on — it has only the statistical aggregation of training examples. The "silence shortcut" is the clearest expression of this: a detector can achieve 99% accuracy by learning a correlation that has nothing to do with speech synthesis at all.

The practical consequence is that **detection is increasingly an arms race rather than an analytic discipline**. The Hunt–Black era produced cost functions still cited thirty years later because they captured stable properties of speech. The deepfake era is producing detectors that become obsolete within months, because they capture properties of specific generators.

## Conclusion

The arc from Hunt–Black to In-the-Wild is the arc from a world in which speech synthesis and audio forensics shared a vocabulary — cepstral distance, F0 continuity, spectral discontinuity — to a world in which they do not. Unit selection and splice detection were dual problems: one hid seams, the other found them. Neural synthesis has no seams, and the forensic problem has correspondingly migrated from signal processing into the harder and less settled territory of out-of-distribution generalization. The Müller results suggest the community has systematically overestimated its own progress by overfitting to ASVspoof, and that the meaningful frontier is not better architectures but better benchmarks and better accounts of why models fail. The thirty-year story is ultimately one of **detection methods outliving their generators, until they suddenly do not** — and the present moment is one in which the gap between laboratory EER and deployed EER is the single most honest metric the field has.

The practical implication for anyone building or relying on audio authentication in 2026 is to treat in-domain benchmark performance as a necessary but profoundly insufficient signal. Deploying a detector that reports 1% EER on ASVspoof into a pipeline of podcast audio, compressed social-media clips, or multilingual political speech will, based on the Fraunhofer and ML-ITW evidence, yield error rates an order of magnitude worse. The field's next legitimate breakthrough will likely come not from a new architecture but from a training-and-evaluation methodology that makes the "difference" gap measurable before deployment rather than after.
